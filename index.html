What states changed on data centers in 2026

Five states, four venues: legislatures, governors, utility regulators, and local governments. Each state moved, and each moved through a different door.

Current as of October 5, 2026. Covers Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, Maryland, and Georgia, the same five states as the data center incentive comparison.

The first comparison looked at what these states give data centers. The second looked at whether states check what they give. This one looks at what changed in 2026, and which part of government produced each change.

What stands out

Governors moved faster than legislatures

Four of the five governors acted outside the legislative process in 2026: executive orders in Pennsylvania, Virginia, and Maryland, and a directive pausing new exemptions in Ohio. Legislatures enacted broad changes in two states, Virginia and Maryland. Pennsylvania's legislature enacted only reporting and load-forecast oversight provisions; both chambers passed exemption repeal language, but in separate bills that were never reconciled. In Georgia, the chambers passed different bills, and neither became law.

The exemptions survived, with strings

No state repealed its data center sales tax exemption in 2026. Instead, states attached conditions or added costs around it: a consumption tax in Virginia, GRID Requirements for new applicants in Pennsylvania, a pause in Ohio, and a repeal effort set for 2027 in Maryland.

Cost allocation is converging on the same tools

Whether by statute, tariff, or executive order, states are reaching for similar mechanisms: size thresholds of 25 or 100 megawatts, minimum bills, long-term contracts, exit fees, and collateral. What differs is who sets them. The durability of each approach will be tested in court in Ohio and at PJM, where Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, and Maryland all sit.

The question I expect to shape 2027: how much of this year's change holds when it rests on executive action rather than statute. An executive order can be revised by the next governor; a law has to be amended by the next legislature.
